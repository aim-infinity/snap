How to capture competitor onboarding flows for a design review

Capture competitor onboarding flows with useful context. Follow a repeatable research workflow, record observations, and prepare evidence for a design review.

By Infinity Labs · October 20, 2026 · 5 min read

You’ve looked at a few onboarding flows and saved the screens you liked.

A few days later, you open them for a review. You remember the layout, but not why one product asked for your role before letting you in—or what happened after the welcome message.

The screenshots survived. Some of the useful context didn’t.

To document competitor onboarding flows, follow a defined task through each product, capture the meaningful states in order, and add an observation while each decision is still fresh. That gives your team a sequence they can discuss, rather than a collection of screens they have to interpret.

Begin with one question your team needs to answer

“Let’s look at onboarding” can turn into a long browsing session with little to bring back.

Choose a question connected to your own design decision. For example:

  • What can a new user do before inviting teammates?
  • Which setup questions can users skip?
  • How does the product explain an empty workspace?
  • What happens when someone doesn’t have data to import?

Suppose your team is deciding whether workspace setup should come before the first task. Your research question could be: What must a new user complete before creating their first project?

That question tells you where to look, what to capture, and when to stop.

Use a comparable starting point

A fresh account may see a different journey from someone joining an existing workspace. A trial account may also get different prompts from a paid account.

Record the context before comparing products:

Field What to record
Research date When you observed the flow
Entry point Homepage signup, invitation, or another route
Account state New account, returning user, or invited member
Plan Free, trial, or paid, where visible
Device Browser and viewport used
Task The outcome you’re trying to reach

If a product requires a different route, note the difference. You can still learn from it, but the comparison needs that context.

Capture decisions as well as polished screens

A welcome screen can be useful. So can the less memorable screen that asks for company size or blocks progress until someone invites a colleague.

For a first-project journey, look for these moments:

  1. The entry screen and its promise.
  2. Account creation and any verification step.
  3. Role, team or workspace questions.
  4. Required setup and optional choices.
  5. The empty state or template selection.
  6. The first created project and the suggested next action.

This is a guide, not a requirement to find six screens in every product. Follow the journey you actually encounter.

Capture a skip option before using it. If you explore a second branch, label it clearly or keep it in a separate flow. Mixing two routes can make the final sequence look like a journey no user actually took.

Add observations while the experience is fresh

A note such as “nice screen” won’t give your team much to discuss later.

Write down what the screen asks the user to do and what that means for your research question. Keep facts and interpretations separate.

Here’s a fictional example:

Observation Interpretation to investigate
Team invitations have a visible Skip option. A solo user can continue without preparing a team list.
A template is selected before project creation. This might reduce the work needed to start; it needs testing with our users.
The empty state offers one primary action. This gives the user a clear next step, though we haven’t measured its effectiveness.

The first column records evidence. The second helps generate questions or hypotheses.

Avoid turning a screenshot into a performance claim. You can observe that a flow has fewer required fields. You can’t conclude from that alone that it converts better.

Organize one flow per product and route

With Snap, you can capture the relevant UI states into a flow as you browse, then add step names, notes and annotations.

Use a name that explains what you investigated, such as Product A — new account to first project — 20 October 2026. For a different route, create a separate flow: Product A — joining by invitation.

Name steps by what happens: “Choose a template” is more informative than “Screenshot 04.” Add your observation beside the step while you still remember the choice you made.

Before the review, remove accidental duplicates and check the order. Use Download PDF to export the sequence with a page for each step, including its title and notes. Attach the document to the review material your team already uses.

Bring a comparison, not just a stack of exports

The captured flows are your evidence. A short comparison helps the team use it.

For each product, summarize the required setup, optional steps, first useful outcome and any unresolved questions. Define what you mean by a “step”: counting screens is different from counting user actions.

Then return to the decision you started with. For example:

Two of the three routes we examined allow project creation before team invitations. We should test whether making invitations optional helps our solo users finish setup.

That is a hypothetical research conclusion, not a finding about specific products. Use the structure with your own observations.

The goal is to leave the review with a design question worth testing. Copying a competitor’s sequence without understanding your users’ needs won’t answer it.

A checklist before the review

  • Can someone follow each route from beginning to end?
  • Are the account state, date and research question recorded?
  • Are optional and required actions clearly distinguished?
  • Have you separated observations from assumptions?
  • Are branch changes and missing screens explained?
  • Does the comparison lead back to a decision your team needs to make?

A useful research capture lets you revisit both the screen and the reason you saved it.
